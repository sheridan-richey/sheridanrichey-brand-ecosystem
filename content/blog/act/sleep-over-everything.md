The client dinner in Troy ran late. My flight home was early the next morning, and the airport was an hour away by Uber.

The old me would have skipped everything and stumbled through the day. Instead I got up and did a short mobility session, packed, and was ready when the driver pulled up. At the airport I walked laps before heading to the lounge, banking steps while I still had the energy. On the plane I skipped the drink service, put my head back, and slept for most of the flight.

That plane nap took my sleep score from the low 40s to 72. Not great. Not ideal. But schedules don't always allow for ideal. What it bought me mattered more than the number: a solid, productive day at home and a near-max activity score, instead of landing wrecked and writing off the rest of the day.

That morning was a small decision. It was also the clearest picture of what this year of travel taught me.

## The trips stacked up

From late May through October, travel stopped being an occasional disruption and started to feel like the default.

Norway for nearly two weeks. Amsterdam for a board meeting, with a side trip to Vilnius. A family vacation in Madison and Chicago. Santiago for a week-long Global Tech Summit with my team. Atlanta two weeks later for another summit. Moncton, a trip I had hoped to avoid that came up at the last minute. A day in Orange County. Then Troy.

No single trip broke my routine. They compounded. And the long ones, the trips that ran more than four days, did something sneaky: they swallowed weekends. Weekends are when I reset, journal, and plan the week ahead. Lose those, and everything else starts to wobble.

## What broke first

Sleep and recovery were the hardest part, every time.

I leaned on AI here, and it paid off. I worked with Claude to build a jetlag plan for the long trips: what to do before takeoff, how to handle the flight, and what to do after landing. Light exposure. Meal timing. Caffeine. When to sleep on the plane and when to stay awake. The biggest lessons were on arrival. Get outside, get some light, move your body in the new place. Do not nap in the middle of the day. Push through until a reasonable local bedtime.

It worked. I handled the time zones better than I ever have.

But protecting sleep had a cost. Something had to give, and what gave was my journaling and planning. Exercise and eating held up pretty well, not perfectly. The reflective work quietly disappeared.

I felt it. Those practices are where my calm and confidence come from. Without them I was getting through the days instead of steering them.

## The routine was too heavy

Here's the honest realization: the problem wasn't travel. Travel just exposed it.

My regimen was built for a predictable life at home. It had a lot of pieces, and every piece assumed time and space I didn't have on the road. A routine that only works when nothing changes isn't a foundation. It's a fair-weather habit.

So I'm working on a lighter version, one I can actually carry.

## The lighter stack

Right now it looks like this, in priority order:

1. **Sleep over everything.** When something has to give, it isn't sleep.
2. **Simple movement.** On hard days, exercise means mobility, movement, a little practice, and getting my steps in. That counts.
3. **Good food choices.** I keep up my intermittent fasting, lean toward fish, and skip red meat. Not perfect, just consistent.
4. **Minimal journaling, with help.** I've cut the practice down, and I lean on AI agents to hold and manage my plans. It's much easier than carrying it all in my head or a notebook on the road.
5. **Plan before the trip.** I do the heavier planning ahead of time, so on the road I only have to deal with what's right in front of me.

## Still figuring it out

The long trips are still the hard case. When work travel eats the weekends I normally use to reset, the lighter stack helps, but it doesn't fully replace that time. How to plan ahead for a ten-day trip so that it holds together is what I'm experimenting with now.

If your calendar looks like mine did this year, start where I did. Pick the one thing you will protect no matter what. For me it's sleep. Make everything else smaller until it fits in a suitcase.
